# Strategy Canvas Guide

## What Is a Strategy Canvas?

A Strategy Canvas is a visual tool from Blue Ocean Strategy (W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne) that captures the current competitive landscape in a single picture. It shows:
- **What factors** the industry competes on (horizontal axis)
- **How much** each competitor invests/delivers on each factor (vertical axis)

When you draw multiple competitors on the same canvas, their "value curves" reveal where the industry has converged (everyone does the same thing) and where differentiation exists.

## Why It Matters

**Convergence = Red Ocean:** When all competitors' curves look similar, the industry is competing on the same factors at similar levels. This leads to price wars and shrinking margins.

**Divergence = Opportunity:** When you draw a dramatically DIFFERENT value curve (high where others are low, zero where others invest heavily), you've found a potential Blue Ocean.

## How to Build a Strategy Canvas

### Step 1: Identify Competitive Factors (8-12)

Competitive factors are what the industry invests in and delivers to customers. They're NOT just product features—they include the full experience.

**Categories to consider:**

**Product/Service factors:**
- Core feature quality
- Feature variety/range
- Customization options
- Reliability/durability
- Speed/performance
- Innovation/newness

**Experience factors:**
- Ease of purchase/setup
- Learning curve
- Customer service quality
- After-sales support
- Brand prestige/status
- Community/ecosystem

**Economic factors:**
- Purchase price
- Total cost of ownership
- Payment flexibility
- Value for money

**Access factors:**
- Availability/distribution
- Geographic reach
- Device/platform support
- Inclusivity (language, accessibility)

**Tips for factor selection:**
- Include factors the industry obsesses over AND factors it ignores
- Ask: "What do existing customers complain about?" (likely an underdelivered factor)
- Ask: "What do non-customers cite as a barrier?" (likely a missing or over-complicated factor)
- Don't include factors that are identical across ALL competitors (table stakes like "basic safety")—unless you plan to eliminate them

### Step 2: Map Competitors (3-5)

Select representative competitors—not necessarily the top 5 by revenue, but competitors that represent DIFFERENT strategies in the market.

**Include:**
- Market leader (highest share)
- Disruptive entrant (if one exists)
- Budget/value player
- Premium player
- Niche/specialized player

For each competitor, rate each factor from 1 (Low investment/delivery) to 5 (High investment/delivery).

**Rating guide:**
- **1 (Very Low):** Barely present or actively eliminated
- **2 (Low):** Below industry average, minimal investment
- **3 (Medium):** At industry average, standard delivery
- **4 (High):** Above average, clear investment
- **5 (Very High):** Industry-leading, major differentiator

### Step 3: Draw and Analyze

**Plot the curves:**
Each competitor becomes a line across the factors. When lines overlap heavily, the industry has converged.

**Look for:**

**Convergence zones:** Factors where everyone scores 3-4. These are "industry orthodoxy"—assumptions everyone shares. They're prime candidates for Eliminate or Reduce.

**Overinvestment:** Factors where everyone scores 4-5 but customers don't actually value them that much. Classic waste.

**Underinvestment:** Factors where everyone scores 1-2. These might be genuine non-priorities OR blind spots hiding opportunity.

**Differentiation attempts:** Factors where ONE competitor scores differently. Are they succeeding? Why?

## Reading the Canvas: Pattern Recognition

### Pattern 1: Flat Line
All competitors score similarly across most factors.
**Meaning:** Red ocean. Industry competing on execution, not strategy. Ripe for Blue Ocean disruption.

### Pattern 2: Two Clusters
Two groups of competitors with distinct curves (e.g., premium vs. budget).
**Meaning:** Market is segmented but within each segment, it's a red ocean. Opportunity may exist BETWEEN segments.

### Pattern 3: One Outlier
One competitor has a dramatically different curve.
**Meaning:** Someone is already attempting a Blue Ocean. Study what they Eliminated/Reduced/Raised/Created. Are they succeeding?

### Pattern 4: All High
Most competitors score 4-5 on most factors.
**Meaning:** Feature war. Costs are high, margins are thin. Customers may be overwhelmed by features they don't use. Strong candidate for Eliminate/Reduce strategy.

## From Strategy Canvas to Four Actions

The canvas directly informs the ERRC grid:

**Factors where everyone invests heavily but customers shrug → ELIMINATE**
"Nobody actually uses the hotel minibar. Eliminate it."

**Factors where everyone invests heavily but 'good enough' would do → REDUCE**
"Nobody needs 200-channel cable. Reduce to 20 great channels."

**Factors where everyone underdelivers but customers care deeply → RAISE**
"Everyone has slow checkout. Raise to instant checkout."

**Factors that don't exist on the canvas but non-customers need → CREATE**
"Nobody offers self-service kiosks. Create them for speed and cost savings."

## Strategy Canvas for Different Industries

### Service Industries
Include factors like: wait time, personalization, ambiance, expertise, availability (hours), booking ease, follow-up quality

### Technology Products
Include factors like: setup time, learning curve, integration breadth, API quality, documentation, community support, update frequency, price per seat

### Consumer Goods
Include factors like: packaging, shelf appeal, ingredient quality, sustainability, brand status, availability, portion/size options, price per unit

### Financial Services
Include factors like: approval speed, interest rate, transparency, mobile access, customer support, qualification requirements, product range, branch network

## Common Mistakes

1. **Too few factors (3-4):** Not enough resolution to see patterns. Aim for 8-12.
2. **Only product features:** Include experience, economic, and access factors too.
3. **Rating based on marketing claims:** Rate based on actual delivery, not what competitors SAY they do.
4. **Including table stakes:** Don't include factors that literally every product must have (like "doesn't catch fire"). Focus on competitive factors.
5. **Only looking at direct competitors:** Include substitutes and alternatives that non-customers use instead.
