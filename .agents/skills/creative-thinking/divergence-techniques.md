# Divergence Techniques

Concrete methods for generating genuinely different options, not just staring at a blank page hoping for range. Pick two or three of these per task, not all of them — the point is real variety between the resulting options, not exhausting every technique.

## Quantity first, judgment later

Generate a set (aim for at least 6-8 for anything that matters) before evaluating any of them — write them as a flat list, one line each, no elaboration yet. Committing to elaborating the first promising-looking idea before the list is done is the single most common way this collapses back into "just the average idea, slightly dressed up." The quantity constraint itself does real work: the first few ideas on any list tend to be the obvious/average ones, and distinctive options tend to show up further down, once the obvious ones are already spent.

## Constraint injection

Open-ended prompts tend to produce average answers because there's nothing forcing a specific direction; a self-imposed constraint breaks the symmetry. Pick an arbitrary-but-specific constraint and generate an idea that satisfies it:
- A specific limitation: "the game has no text at all," "the play has exactly two characters and one set," "the design uses only two colors."
- A specific inversion: "what if the usual protagonist is instead the antagonist's ally," "what if the tutorial IS the final boss fight."
- A specific borrowed rule from an unrelated domain (see Analogical Transfer below).

Generate several ideas under different constraints rather than one constraint applied once — the constraints themselves are disposable scaffolding; keep only what's actually interesting about the resulting idea, not the constraint itself.

## SCAMPER (for improving/varying an existing concept)

A structured checklist for generating variations on something that already exists (a mechanic, a scene, a design), useful once there's a baseline idea to push against rather than for a first idea from nothing:
- **S**ubstitute — swap a core component for something else (a different resource, a different character role, a different material).
- **C**ombine — merge this concept with an unrelated one (see Forced Combination below).
- **A**dapt — borrow a solution from a different context that solves an analogous problem.
- **M**odify/magnify — exaggerate one dimension of it (much bigger, much smaller, much faster, much slower) and see what that implies.
- **P**ut to another use — what else could this same mechanic/asset/structure be repurposed for.
- **E**liminate — remove a component that seems essential and see what's forced to fill the gap.
- **R**everse — invert the order, the roles, or the goal.

## Forced combination

Pick two unrelated concepts and force them together, then work out what a coherent result would actually look like (the forcing is the point — don't pick two things that already go together naturally, that's not divergence). Draw the second concept from somewhere genuinely outside the immediate domain: a different genre, an unrelated field (cooking, architecture, a sport), a random noun. "A brick-breaker where the bricks are also musical notes you're composing" is forced combination; "a brick-breaker with power-ups" is not (power-ups are already the domain default).

## Reversal / inversion

Take the assumption everyone would make by default and invert it, then see what a coherent idea looks like under the inverted assumption:
- Invert the goal (instead of winning by destroying things, win by preserving them).
- Invert the power dynamic (the small/weak character is actually the one in control).
- Invert the audience's knowledge relative to the character's (the audience knows something the protagonist doesn't, or vice versa, more strongly than the default).

Not every inversion produces something good — that's expected; the point of generating several is that most get discarded, and the discarding is normal, not a sign the technique failed.

## Analogical transfer

Borrow the *structure* of a solution from a completely different domain and reapply it. This works because the interesting part of an idea is often a structural pattern, not the surface content it originally showed up in — a pacing pattern from music (build, breakdown, drop) can shape a level's difficulty curve; a negotiation structure from a totally different kind of story can shape a scene's back-and-forth. Explicitly name the source domain and what structural pattern is being borrowed, so it's clear what's actually being transferred versus just vaguely "inspired by."

## "Worst possible idea" as a warm-up

Deliberately generate a few obviously bad or absurd ideas first, with no self-censoring. This isn't wasted output — it loosens the tendency to only propose "safe" ideas, and a bad idea often contains one genuinely interesting fragment worth extracting and recombining with something else (see [Selection & Refinement](selection-and-refinement.md)) even though the idea as a whole doesn't work.

## Recognizing when a set isn't actually diverged yet

Before moving to selection, check the list: if every option is a small variation on the same underlying idea (same setting, same core mechanic, same structure, just renamed), the divergence didn't really happen — apply another technique rather than proceeding to select among near-duplicates.
