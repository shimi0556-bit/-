# Selection & Refinement

Divergence produces options; this is the discipline for choosing well among them instead of defaulting to whichever is easiest to execute or arrived first.

## Don't select for ease of execution

The easiest idea to build is not the same axis as the strongest idea — conflating them is how a genuinely interesting option quietly loses to a safe one. Evaluate ideas on their own merit first (does this actually serve the goal, is it specific and distinctive, does it hold up under a moment's scrutiny), and only bring in feasibility/effort as a second, separate filter afterward. An idea that's slightly harder to execute but meaningfully better is usually worth the extra effort, especially for anything the user will judge on originality (see the scaling guidance in the main skill).

## What to actually look for

- **Specificity**: could this idea's description apply to many other projects with the names changed, or is it recognizably about *this* one? Prefer the latter.
- **A genuine reason, not just novelty**: an idea should be interesting *because* of how it serves the actual goal (the game's core loop, the play's central conflict, the design's subject) — novelty on its own, disconnected from purpose, is the "weird for its own sake" trap noted in [Avoiding the Default](avoiding-the-default.md).
- **Holds up under one layer of "why"**: ask why this idea works, once. If the honest answer is "it's unusual," that's thin; if the answer connects back to the core goal, it's sturdier.
- **Emotional or conceptual specificity over safety**: between two similarly executable ideas, the one that takes a clearer, more particular stance is usually the better choice, even if it's slightly more disputable.

## Combine fragments rather than picking one idea wholesale

The strongest final concept is often not any single generated option but a combination of the best fragment from two or three of them — a mechanic from one idea paired with the tone of another, a structure from one paired with a detail from a third. Don't treat the divergence list as a multiple-choice question with exactly one right answer; treat it as raw material. This is also where a "worst possible idea" (see [Divergence Techniques](divergence-techniques.md)) often pays off — the idea as a whole gets discarded, but one fragment of it turns out to be exactly what a stronger idea needed.

## Cut the average options explicitly

When narrowing a list, actively discard the options that turned out to be close to the obvious default (per [Avoiding the Default](avoiding-the-default.md)) even if they're competent — don't let a safe, unremarkable option survive into the final round just because nothing is technically wrong with it. A shorter list of genuinely distinct, defensible options is more useful than a longer list padded with safe filler.

## Getting a second read

Where feasible, evaluate the shortlist as if seeing it fresh rather than from inside the process that generated it — the option that felt like a "eureka" while generating it can look ordinary a few minutes later, and vice versa. A brief pause, or explicitly re-reading the shortlist against the original goal from scratch, catches cases where attachment to the effort of generating an idea is doing the selecting instead of the idea's actual merit.

## Knowing when to stop refining

Selection and refinement can also over-run — endlessly second-guessing a genuinely strong, specific choice in search of an even better one has diminishing returns past a point, especially under real time constraints. Once an idea passes the specificity and "genuine reason" checks above and no remaining option in the set is clearly stronger, commit to it rather than continuing to diverge indefinitely.
