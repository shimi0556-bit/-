Instructions for producing ideas that are actually distinctive, not just plausible. This is a meta-skill — it doesn't replace the domain skills in this project ([game-design-building](../game-design-building/SKILL.md), [playwriting](../playwriting/SKILL.md), [course-presenter-layout](../course-presenter-layout/SKILL.md)) but sits underneath all of them at the ideation stage, and applies just as much to naming, copywriting, visual concepts, or any other open-ended creative choice.

## The core problem this addresses

A language model's default output for an open-ended creative prompt tends toward the statistically average answer for that kind of prompt — the response most similar to the bulk of its training examples, because that's the safest, most probable completion. A study analyzing AI brainstorming sessions found roughly 73% of AI-generated ideas were generic and predictable, converging on the same narrow set of answers different people and different tools would independently produce. This isn't a one-off failure to fix with a better prompt each time — it's the default gravitational pull of the process, worth actively counteracting on every creative task, not just the ones that feel obviously stuck.

Concretely: if the first idea that comes to mind for "a fantasy adventure game" is an elf/dwarf/human party fighting a dark lord, or the first concept for "a birthday card" is balloons and a cake — that's the average, not a choice. It's not wrong, but it's not a creative decision either; it's the path of least resistance. See [Avoiding the Default](avoiding-the-default.md) for how to recognize this pull in the moment and specific techniques to break out of it.

## The discipline: diverge, then converge — never skip straight to "good enough"

1. **Generate wide before judging.** Produce several genuinely different directions — not several small variations on one idea — before evaluating any of them. Judging the first idea as it arrives shuts down the divergent phase before it starts; the second and third ideas are often still near the average, so a real set of options usually needs more than three. See [Divergence Techniques](divergence-techniques.md) for concrete methods (constraint injection, forced combination, reversal, analogical transfer) rather than staring at a blank page hoping for range.
2. **Push past the first idea deliberately**, even when it's good — the goal isn't to discard it, it's to confirm it's actually the strongest option rather than just the first one reached for. If every option in the set resembles the first idea, the divergence step didn't really happen; go back and apply a technique that forces a genuinely different angle, not a cosmetic variant.
3. **Select and refine deliberately**, not by taking whichever idea is easiest to execute. See [Selection & Refinement](selection-and-refinement.md) for what to actually look for when choosing among a wide set, and how to combine strong fragments from multiple ideas rather than picking one wholesale.

## When to invest in this vs. when not to

Scale the ideation effort to what the task actually calls for:
- **A small, well-scoped creative choice** (one line of dialogue, a single UI copy string, a minor palette choice) doesn't need a full divergent pass — use judgment, not ceremony.
- **Anything the user will judge on originality** (a game's hook, a play's premise, a brand/visual identity, a name, a headline, a design direction) benefits from at least one real divergence pass before committing, even under time pressure — a rushed but genuinely varied set of 3-4 options beats one polished generic one.
- **When the user gives a specific creative direction**, honor it exactly — divergence is for filling in what they left open, not for second-guessing an explicit choice they already made.

## Applying this alongside domain skills

This skill's techniques feed the ideation moments inside other work, rather than running as a separate step every time:
- Naming a game's "one distinguishing mechanic" ([game-design-building](../game-design-building/design-fundamentals.md#the-one-distinguishing-mechanic)) is exactly the kind of choice that rewards divergence before commitment.
- A play's premise and central dramatic question ([playwriting](../playwriting/dramatic-structure.md)) is a high-leverage place to generate real alternatives rather than the first premise that comes to mind.
- Visual/artifact design already has its own explicit list of AI-generated-look clichés to avoid (warm cream + serif + terracotta, purple-to-blue gradient heroes, `rounded-lg` everywhere, and the like) — that's this same phenomenon applied specifically to visual design; treat it as a worked example of [Avoiding the Default](avoiding-the-default.md), not a separate concern.
