# Hooks & Retention

The first few seconds decide whether the rest of the video gets watched at all — on a scrollable feed, a viewer can leave in under a second, so the hook isn't an intro, it's the highest-leverage part of the whole edit.

## The open-loop hook

A hook works by opening a loop the viewer needs closed — a question they need answered, a claim they need to see tested, a stake they can't ignore. It is not a logo, a channel intro, a slow establishing shot, or a throat-clearing "hey guys" — all of those are dead time before the actual hook, and on short-form specifically, dead time before the hook is often fatal to retention. Concretely, a hook usually does one of:
- States a specific, surprising claim ("this took 4,000 hours down to 800").
- Poses a question the video will answer.
- Shows the *result* first, then the video explains how (works especially well for build/process content — open on the finished thing, not step one).
- Creates visible tension or a stake ("if this doesn't work, the whole thing breaks").

A weak hook is usually vague where a strong one is specific — "let me show you something cool" opens no real loop; "I broke my own record by removing one line of code" does.

## The hook → promise → payoff shape

A workable default structure for anything with real retention pressure (short-form especially, but useful as a checklist for longer content too):
1. **Hook (first 1-3 seconds)**: open the loop, at the video's highest energy — this is not the place to ease in.
2. **Promise (next few seconds)**: make explicit (or strongly implicit) what the viewer will get if they keep watching — confirms the hook wasn't a bait-and-switch, which matters for both retention and trust.
3. **Value delivery (the bulk of the video)**: build with escalating interest rather than flat delivery — see [Pacing & Rhythm](pacing-and-rhythm.md) for keeping this section from sagging in the middle.
4. **Payoff (the end)**: close the loop the hook opened. A video that opens a loop and never closes it reads as unsatisfying even if the content along the way was good.

## One idea per clip/section

Especially for short-form: a clip trying to cover three separate points usually lands none of them as memorably as a clip built around one clear idea. When a concept genuinely needs multiple beats, make sure each beat has its own small hook/turn rather than presenting them as one undifferentiated block.

## Captions and text overlay affect retention, not just accessibility

On muted/sound-off viewing (extremely common on social platforms), on-screen text carries the hook — a strong verbal hook with no caption support loses a large share of viewers who never unmute. Keep hook-critical text large, on-screen early, and readable at a glance; see [Remotion Captions](../remotion-captions/SKILL.md) for implementation, and don't treat captions as an accessibility afterthought added at the end — for short-form specifically, they're load-bearing for the hook itself.

## Matching promise to actual payoff

Retention research and platform algorithms both punish (via completion/rewatch signals) a hook that oversells what the video actually delivers — a strong hook that doesn't pay off tends to hurt future distribution more than a weaker but honest one. Don't inflate a hook's claim beyond what Step 4 (payoff) can actually deliver.

## Format-specific retention notes

- **Short-form (TikTok/Reels/Shorts)**: assume near-zero patience — the hook must land in the first 1-3 seconds, with the promise clear by second 5. See [Pacing & Rhythm](pacing-and-rhythm.md) for the matching cut frequency.
- **Mid-length / YouTube**: the hook still matters in the first several seconds, but there's more room after it — a strong hook can be followed by a brief, deliberate setup before the main content, as long as it doesn't drift into unrelated preamble.
- **Long-form / sought-out (a course lesson someone opened deliberately)**: retention pressure is lower since the viewer already opted in, but a clear hook (what this lesson will let you do, stated up front) still improves completion — don't skip it just because the format is more forgiving.
