Instructions for writing a stage play script — dialogue and stage directions meant to be performed live in front of an audience, not filmed. This is a distinct discipline from screenwriting: a stage has no cuts, no close-ups, and a fixed physical space the audience watches continuously, which shapes structure, dialogue, and stage directions differently than for film/TV.

## When to use

Writing an original play or adapting a story into one — a full-length play, a one-act, a school production, a community-theater piece, a sketch/scene for a class. Not for a screenplay (film/TV format and constraints differ — camera cuts, scene headings like INT./EXT. — those don't apply here), and not for a slide-presentation narration script (see [Course Presenter Layout](../course-presenter-layout/SKILL.md) for that instead).

## Step 1 — Establish the premise before writing dialogue

Get these decided first, even briefly — they shape everything downstream:

- **Premise**: what's the central dramatic question or conflict? ("Can she forgive her father before he dies" is a premise; "a family reunion happens" is not — it has no question driving it.)
- **Protagonist's want vs. need**: what the character consciously pursues (want) vs. what they actually need to change or learn (need) — the gap between them is usually where the drama lives.
- **Setting and time constraints**: how many locations, how much time passes. A play that stays in one room across one evening is far cheaper and tighter to stage than one hopping across five locations and twenty years — decide this deliberately, not by accident (see [Stagecraft Constraints](format-and-stagecraft.md) before locking it in, especially for school/community productions with real budget and cast limits).
- **Length/form**: one-act (roughly 10–40 minutes, single sitting, no intermission) vs. full-length (60–120+ minutes, usually with an intermission and multiple acts). A one-act should resolve one central conflict tightly; don't pad it with a subplot that needs a full-length's room to breathe.

## Step 2 — Structure

Read [Dramatic Structure](dramatic-structure.md) for act/scene shaping, the objective-obstacle-tactic model for building individual scenes, and where turning points belong. Build an outline (even a rough beat list of "what changes in each scene") before drafting full dialogue — discovering the structure is broken after 40 pages of dialogue is expensive to fix; discovering it in a 1-page outline is cheap.

## Step 3 — Character and dialogue

Read [Dialogue & Character](dialogue-and-character.md) for voice differentiation, subtext, handling exposition without it sounding like exposition, and dialogue economy. The single highest-leverage craft check: cover the character names and read a page of dialogue — if you can't tell who's speaking from word choice and rhythm alone, the voices haven't been differentiated yet.

## Step 4 — Stage directions and format

Read [Format & Stagecraft](format-and-stagecraft.md) for the standard stage-play script format (distinct from screenplay format) and for writing stage directions that describe *actions*, not camera moves or an actor's inner emotional state. This file also covers practical staging constraints (cast size, entrances/exits, scene-change cost, doubling roles) and free tools for formatting a script properly.

## Step 5 — Table read and revise

Before calling a draft done, read it aloud (or have it read aloud, ideally by more than one voice) — dialogue that looks fine on the page frequently reveals itself as unspeakable, unclear, or the wrong length only when spoken. Listen specifically for:
- Lines that are grammatically fine but no human would actually say under pressure/emotion.
- A scene that reads as flat when performed even though it looked eventful on paper — usually means the underlying conflict (want vs. obstacle) wasn't strong enough, not a line-level fix.
- Runtime — a rough rule of thumb is about 1 page of standard-format dialogue per minute of stage time; use a read-aloud pass to sanity check the estimate, especially for a play with a strict time limit (festival one-acts, school assembly slots).
