# Dialogue & Character

Dialogue is the entire medium a stage play has for character, plot, and subtext — there's no camera to cut to a meaningful glance, no narration. Every line is doing more work than the equivalent line in prose.

## Voice differentiation

The single best test: cover the character names on a page of dialogue and read it. If you can't tell who's speaking from word choice, sentence length, and rhythm alone, the voices aren't differentiated yet. Concrete levers:

- **Sentence length and completeness**: one character speaks in short fragments, another in long, qualifying, run-on sentences that hedge everything.
- **Vocabulary register**: formal vs. slang, technical jargon vs. plain language, a specific verbal tic or phrase a character returns to (used sparingly — overuse turns a tic into a gimmick).
- **What they talk about, unprompted**: a character who redirects every conversation toward money, or toward themselves, or who deflects with humor, is characterized by that pattern independent of any single line.
- **Rhythm of exchange**: a character who answers questions directly vs. one who answers a different question than the one asked (deflection), or answers with another question.

Avoid giving every character the playwright's own natural cadence by default — deliberately push each character's voice away from your own before deciding it's "how they'd say it."

## Subtext — saying one thing, meaning another

Real conflict-driven dialogue is rarely characters stating their feelings directly. A character who wants reassurance often won't ask for it outright; a character avoiding a hard truth changes the subject. Write the *literal* line, but know what the character is actually pursuing underneath it (their scene objective, per [Dramatic Structure](dramatic-structure.md)) — the gap between the literal line and the real want is what makes dialogue feel true rather than expository.

A useful check: if a line could only be delivered one way (there's no tension between what's said and what's meant), consider whether it's doing enough work, or whether it's stating the theme/emotion directly instead of dramatizing it.

## Avoiding "on-the-nose" dialogue

On-the-nose dialogue says exactly what the scene is about, plainly, with no gap — "I'm so angry at you for leaving me," said flatly in anger. It reads as amateurish because real people rarely state their core feeling that directly, especially under emotional pressure. Fixes:
- Let the emotion come out sideways — through a tactic (sarcasm, a pointed question, changing the subject, an overreaction to something small that's really about the big thing).
- Cut the line that states the theme and trust the scene's action to carry it — if the audience needs a character to literally announce the theme to understand the scene, the scene's action isn't yet doing that job.

## Exposition that doesn't sound like exposition

Never have two characters who both already know a fact explain it to each other purely for the audience's benefit. Instead:
- Reveal information as a byproduct of conflict — an accusation, an argument, a confession forced by circumstance — rather than a calm recap.
- Introduce it through a character who genuinely doesn't know, asking a real question that another character has a real (possibly reluctant) reason to answer.
- Let some backstory stay implied rather than fully explained — audiences tolerate not knowing everything immediately far better than they tolerate a stretch of dialogue that exists only to inform them.

## Economy

Stage dialogue should generally do more with less than prose dialogue — there's no interior narration to add nuance, so trust the actor's delivery and the audience's inference rather than over-explaining:
- Cut lines that restate what the previous line already made clear.
- Prefer a short, sharp exchange (rapid back-and-forth) over one character delivering a long uninterrupted speech, except where a monologue is a deliberate structural choice (a turning point, a confession, a set-piece moment) — used constantly, long speeches slow the play's momentum.
- Read dialogue aloud (see Step 5 of the main skill) — the line that seems fine silently read often turns out too long, too formal, or unspeakable in one breath when actually spoken.

## Distinguishing dialogue tags from stage directions

Keep dialogue itself free of authorial commentary about *how* to say it wherever the line's own content already implies the delivery — "she said angrily" as a parenthetical crutch usually means the line itself isn't yet doing the work of conveying anger. Reserve bracketed emotional/tone cues for genuine ambiguity, not as a default habit; see [Format & Stagecraft](format-and-stagecraft.md) for how these are formatted when they are needed.
