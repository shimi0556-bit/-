# Format & Stagecraft

## Standard stage-play script format

Stage-play format is a distinct convention from screenplay format (no INT./EXT. scene headings, no camera directions) — theater conventions instead:

**Title page**: title, author, and usually a short line noting it's unpublished/for consideration, plus contact info if submitting anywhere.

**Cast of characters**: a list before the script proper — name, brief description (age, one line of who they are), and often the setting/time period, listed once so a reader/director doesn't have to infer it from dialogue.

**Act/Scene headers**: e.g. `ACT ONE`, `Scene 1`, followed by a short setting description in italics (time, place, what the stage looks like at rise) — enough for a director to picture the set, not a full prose description.

**Character cues**: character name in ALL CAPS, centered or left-aligned depending on house style, on its own line, immediately followed by their dialogue — no blank line between the name and the line.

```
MARGARET
You said you'd be here by six.

DAVID
(checking his watch)
It's five fifty-eight.
```

**Stage directions**:
- In parentheses and italicized when they interrupt or precede a line of dialogue (a small action, a tone cue used sparingly — see [Dialogue & Character](dialogue-and-character.md) on not over-using tone cues).
- Set apart from dialogue visually (commonly indented further, e.g. ~2.75" from the left margin) so a reader never confuses a stage direction for a spoken line.
- Describe *physical action or stage business*, not camera moves (there is no camera) and not an actor's internal emotional state as an instruction to "play" — describe what happens, let the actor and director interpret how. ("He crosses to the window and doesn't turn around" — not "He feels devastated.")
- Character names inside stage directions are also capitalized (e.g. "MARGARET exits.").

**Consistency matters more than which exact convention**: house styles vary slightly (indentation amounts, centered vs. left-aligned names) between publishers, festivals, and countries (UK vs. US convention differs a little) — pick one convention and apply it uniformly through the whole script rather than mixing styles.

## Free tools for formatting

- **[Celtx](https://www.celtx.com/)** — free tier, browser-based, includes a native stage-play format option (distinct from its screenplay format) — a solid free default if the goal is a properly formatted, submission-ready script without hand-rolling the format in a word processor.
- **[Trelby](https://www.trelby.org/)** — free, open-source desktop app. Built primarily around screenplay format, but usable for a stage play with manual adjustment; a reasonable offline-first option.
- **Plain word processor with a manual style template** (Google Docs/LibreOffice/Word) — entirely viable for a first draft: set up character-cue and stage-direction paragraph styles once, reuse them throughout. No dependency, no account, and exports to PDF cleanly — often the simplest path for a single school or community production that isn't being submitted to a formal festival with strict formatting requirements.
- Formal play-submission formatting requirements (for festivals, competitions, or publishers) often have their own house style sheet — when submitting somewhere specific, check that venue's formatting guidelines rather than assuming the general conventions above are sufficient as-is.

## Stagecraft constraints — writing for what a stage can actually do

A script that would be trivial to shoot on film can be expensive or impossible to stage. Decide these deliberately, especially for a school or community production with real budget/cast/crew limits:

- **Cast size**: every named speaking role needs an actual actor. A play with 20 named characters needs either 20 actors or deliberate doubling (see below) — check this against who's actually available before finishing a draft, not after.
- **Doubling roles**: one actor playing multiple characters is a normal, budget-friendly convention, but only works cleanly when those characters are never on stage at the same time and have enough of a costume/vocal distinction that the audience can track the switch. Plan doubling into the structure (which scenes each role appears in) rather than discovering a conflict late.
- **Set/location changes**: a stage can't cut instantly between locations the way film can. Each change of setting either needs a scene break (lights down, set changed, often with a real time cost) or a design solution (a flexible/unit set that suggests multiple locations without literal scenery changes). Minimizing the number of distinct locations, or grouping scenes that share a location, keeps a script easier and cheaper to actually stage.
- **Entrances and exits**: characters must physically arrive and leave via the stage's actual entrances — a character can't simply "be gone" the way a film can cut away. Make sure every entrance/exit is motivated and give characters a plausible route on and off, especially in a single-set play with limited entrance points.
- **Props and physical business**: anything a script calls for (a working phone, a meal being cooked, a fight) has to be genuinely executable, safely, by the cast available, every performance. A staged fight or anything involving real risk (weapons, fire, complex physical stunts) needs a director experienced in stage combat/safety — flag it as needing specialized handling rather than writing detailed violence casually.
- **Runtime awareness**: roughly one script page (standard format) per minute of stage time is a common rule of thumb — useful for sanity-checking a script against a hard time limit (a school assembly slot, a festival's one-act cap), though confirm with an actual read-aloud pass (see Step 5 of the main skill) rather than relying on the page-count estimate alone.
