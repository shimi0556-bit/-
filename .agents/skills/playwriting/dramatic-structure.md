# Dramatic Structure

Structure is what makes a sequence of scenes feel like it's building toward something, rather than just being events in order. Get the shape right before writing full dialogue.

## Acts and scenes

- **Act**: a major structural division, usually marking a significant shift in the situation (often paired with an intermission in full-length plays). Most full-length plays use two or three acts; one-acts are, by definition, a single act.
- **Scene**: a continuous unit of action, usually bounded by a change in time, location, or which characters are present. A scene should have its own internal shape (see objective-obstacle-tactic below) — it isn't just "the next chunk of plot," it's a mini-conflict that resolves or escalates by its end.

Every scene should change something — information the audience has, a character's status, a relationship, or the state of the central conflict. If a scene could be cut entirely without the rest of the play needing adjustment, that's a sign it isn't pulling its weight structurally, however well-written the dialogue in it is.

## The three (or five) act shape, loosely

Useful as a checklist, not a rigid template to force every play into:

1. **Setup** — establish the world, the protagonist, and the central want, and introduce the inciting incident that sets the conflict in motion. This should happen early — a play that spends its first third "just establishing atmosphere" with no forward pressure risks losing the audience before the actual story starts.
2. **Rising action / complications** — the protagonist pursues their want, hits obstacles, and the stakes escalate. Each complication should make the situation *harder to resolve*, not just *different* — repeating the same kind of obstacle reads as padding.
3. **Crisis / climax** — the central conflict comes to its sharpest point, usually forcing the protagonist into a decisive choice or confrontation that can't be undone.
4. **Resolution** — the immediate aftermath; loose ends are addressed (not necessarily happily) and the audience is given a clear sense of where things have landed. Keep this short relative to the rest of the play — a resolution that drags re-diffuses the climax's tension.

## Objective, obstacle, tactic — building a scene

For any given scene, name explicitly:
- **Objective**: what does this character want *in this scene, right now* (may be a smaller, immediate version of their overall want for the play).
- **Obstacle**: what's stopping them — another character's opposing objective, a physical constraint, information they don't have.
- **Tactics**: what the character actually *does* to get past the obstacle — persuade, threaten, charm, lie, withdraw, deflect. A character often shifts tactics mid-scene as earlier ones fail; that shift is frequently where the best dialogue beats live.

A scene where every character wants the same thing, or where nobody is actually trying to get anything, tends to read as flat even if the dialogue is well-written line by line — conflict of objectives is what gives a scene shape.

## Turning points

A turning point is a moment where the direction of the scene (or the play) reverses or shifts significantly — new information lands, a tactic fails and forces a new approach, a decision gets made that can't be walked back. Structurally:
- Every scene benefits from at least one turning point — otherwise it risks reading as a static conversation rather than dramatic action.
- The act/play-level turning points (end of Act One, the crisis) should be the biggest ones — build toward them rather than spending equally large reversals early and leaving nothing left to escalate to.

## Exposition and the "who needs to know what, and when" problem

Backstory and world information should surface because a character needs it *for their present objective* — not delivered as a standalone information-dump scene. If two characters already both know a fact, having them state it to each other purely for the audience's benefit ("as you know, Father, you disowned me five years ago...") reads as unnatural — see [Dialogue & Character](dialogue-and-character.md) for handling this properly. Structurally, the fix is often to introduce a character who genuinely doesn't know the information yet, or to reveal it as a consequence of conflict (an accusation forces someone to admit something) rather than as a scheduled information dump.

## One-act vs. full-length structural differences

- **One-act**: usually one central conflict, minimal subplot, often a single setting/timeframe. Every scene needs to serve the one throughline tightly — there's no room for a structurally separate subplot to develop and pay off.
- **Full-length**: can sustain a subplot (a secondary conflict, often thematically related to the main one) that intersects with the main plot at key structural points (often near the act breaks). Don't let a subplot run for pages without touching the main conflict — audiences lose the thread of why they should care about a fully disconnected storyline.
